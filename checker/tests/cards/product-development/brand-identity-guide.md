# Brand identity guide

Document type in the Product development universe spec (product-development 0.9).

- Also called: brand guidelines / style guide / DESIGN.md
- Helps you: apply the brand's look and wording correctly on your own
- Who uses it: anyone writing or designing for the brand
- When it is used: whenever making new material for the brand
- No document needed when time-separation is none and reversibility is reversible. Depends on: Who has to reconstruct this reasoning without the author present? (time-separation); Can this step be undone, and at what cost? (reversibility)

## How to write it well

- Boundary: Brand values and purpose belong in the Product Vision and the Positioning Statement. Ask whether a passage says what the brand stands for (put it there) or how the brand should feel and be expressed in look and wording (keep it here).
- Heuristic: Brand guidelines, a brand book and a style guide all count as this guide when they set the brand's intended feeling and its rules for look and wording, whatever the document is called.
- Convention: DESIGN.md represents visual design-system rules. It can supply the visual portion of a brand guide; it does not represent its verbal rules.
- Pitfall: Instructions for revising the document are not part of the document. That is a process, and it belongs wherever the process is owned.
- Refresh: Stale when an adopted colour, typeface, logo or other brand asset changes. A guide that shows a look nobody uses any more misleads more confidently than having no guide.

## Sections

### Brand character

Answers: What feeling should the brand's look and wording create?
Required.

- Convention: The opening Overview of a DESIGN.md carries this: personality, audience and the response the brand should evoke. It is what to fall back on when no rule covers a case.

### Visual identity rules

Answers: What are the rules for the brand's look?
Required.

- Boundary: Distinct from Verbal Identity Rules: marks, colour, type and imagery, and how the name is drawn as a logotype, are look; words, voice, naming, standing copy, and how the name is spelled, cased and punctuated in text, are wording. A trademark policy usually splits across both.
- Heuristic: A rule set with nothing forbidden is usually not settled yet.
- Pitfall: Mood boards help a team agree on a feeling before any rule exists, and get copied into the rules by mistake. Record only what was adopted: the final colours, type and marks.
- Convention: The machine-readable form fixes the order the sections come in, and requires none of them except a primary colour palette.
- Convention: Token formats carry colour, size, type and timing. Icons, accessibility and writing rules fit in none of them, and are no less settled for that.
- Heuristic: Name a specific reference rather than a list of adjectives. 'Modern, clean, premium' fits almost anything.
- Boundary: Distinct from Cross-Cutting Concerns: here go the brand's standing rules for its look; a record that one design meets an obligation such as accessibility goes there. Ask whether the line sets a rule for all material or checks one piece of work.
- Empty: Most of these are only partly answered. A settled palette and nothing else is a real answer, not a stub.

### Verbal identity rules

Answers: What are the rules for the brand's wording?
Required.

- Convention: Writing sits among the foundations as a peer of colour and typography, not beneath them.
- Convention: Where the guide is split into a visual branch and a content or messaging branch, as GitLab's Pajamas is, put these rules in the content branch.
- Heuristic: The words a brand refuses do as much work as the ones it adopts.
- Boundary: Distinct from Positioning Statement and Value Proposition: here goes the approved wording itself, such as boilerplate, taglines and pitches of set lengths. Ask whether you are fixing words or deciding what to claim; deciding what to claim belongs there, and the wording here draws on it without restating it.
- Empty: Where wording is decided case by case and no rules exist, note that here, so a reader can tell it apart from rules that exist but have not been collected.

### Positioning statement

Answers: Which kind of offering should each segment see ours as?
When applicable.
Applies only when uptake is chosen or bought. Depends on: How do people come to take up the offering? (uptake)
Kept in another document (Product strategy canvas or Startup canvas or Competitor analysis): link to that document. The notes below are for writing the section in that document.

- Convention: For [segment] who [need], X is a [category] that [benefit], unlike [alternative].
- Pitfall: Brand-led organisations treat this as marketing's asset and product-led ones as product's. Settle ownership before it gets written twice.
- Empty, when maturity is unproven: Pre-competition work often cannot fill the 'unlike' clause honestly. Leave it empty rather than fake it. Depends on: How settled is the bet this work rests on? (maturity)

### Constraints

Answers: What imposed limits apply to the work?
When applicable.

- Heuristic: Imposed, not chosen: regulation, budget, deadline, platform, physics.
- Boundary: Distinct from Cross-Cutting Concerns: a constraint is an imposed limit on the work that no design choice moves — budget, deadline, platform, what the law allows; a cross-cutting concern is an obligation every part of the solution must be designed to meet — security, privacy, accessibility. One law can supply both: a limit here, a property demanded throughout there.
- Boundary: Distinct from Trade-offs. Mislabelling a choice as a constraint hides a decision.

### Open questions

Answers: Which questions about the work remain open?
When applicable.

- Heuristic: Each wants an owner and a resolution path. An unowned question is a risk mislabelled.
- Boundary: Distinct from Risks & Assumptions: an open question is something to answer before a point can be settled; an assumption is a belief the work proceeds on and checks along the way. Proceeding without the answer turns the question into an assumption.
- Heuristic: Compare the list across drafts: it should get shorter. A list that keeps growing means the work is not yet ready to settle.
- Empty: Write 'none open' only when every question raised has been answered or recorded as an assumption, so a reader can tell it apart from a list nobody has drawn up.

## What the conditions mean

- uptake is chosen: Do they take it up only by deciding to, with nothing to pay?
- uptake is bought: Is paying how they take it up?
